# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Players who want a fast browser 2D mining, crafting, survival, and exploration loop with Terraria-like descent, Minecraft-like resource handling, and clear controls. They are usually testing directly in the browser, so the first minutes must make movement, mining, combat, crafting, light, recovery, restart, backpack, camp, and interaction paths obvious.

## Product Purpose

AbyssForge is a playable pixel-art expedition game. Success means the player can spawn safely, dig downward, travel sideways into new procedural country, fight mobs, find secrets, craft upgrades, recover at camp points, and understand why each danger or reward happened.

The world should not feel like one repeated wall of stone. Depth needs readable progression: early routes are forgiving, mid layers trade safety for ore, late layers pressure light and recovery, and abyss seams should open new rules rather than just adding more rows. Horizontal exploration should feel like newly discovered side-country, not a copied strip of starter terrain.

The hidden purpose is important: the game begins as a mining loop, then slowly reveals that cache marks, field notes, mobs, the Watcher, and even some UI events understand the player's presence in the world.

## Current Product Surface

- 33 item types, 65 recipes, and 63 achievements.
- 8 runtime biomes and 5 depth strata with gameplay properties.
- 6 surface region types driven by the WorldGen Director.
- 12 contextual chest loot tables.
- 19 hidden lore notes and 8 long-term hidden goals.
- Repeatable product screenshots generated by `npm run docs:screenshots`.

## Brand Personality

Tactile, dangerous, readable, and a little suspicious. The game should feel like a compact underground expedition that grows into a stranger world, not a decorative landing page or a static tech demo.

## Anti-references

Avoid generic SaaS dashboards, oversized marketing heroes, soft decorative gradients, vague tutorial copy, unreadable dark-on-dark UI, and animations that delay control. Avoid abstract camp services that are not grounded in visible world objects. Avoid procedural content that appears too frequently, too symmetrically, or with no reason tied to biome, distance, depth, light, or story phase.

## Design Principles

- World objects should carry mechanics: lights, campfires, chests, ladders, and hazards must be visible, usable, and testable.
- Safety-critical rules should be explicit: spawn support, range limits, recovery points, and boss locks must never rely on hidden assumptions.
- World systems should be data-driven: strata, surface regions, mob ecology, cave events, rewards, and hazards should be tunable as content, not scattered magic numbers.
- Discovery should be paced: recipes, inventory categories, POI, lore, and strange events should open during play rather than exposing everything at spawn.
- Motion should explain state: hits, recovery, light flicker, pickups, and drawer transitions should communicate cause and feedback.
- Pixel art should remain code-native and consistent unless a raster asset clearly improves gameplay.
- UI should support repeated play: dense enough for survival information, but stable and readable at desktop and small-screen sizes.
- The hidden story should stay subtle: the miner and mobs can seem aware of the player, but the game should avoid over-explaining the meta layer too early.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Keep text contrast high, preserve keyboard controls, maintain visible button states, avoid layout-shifting animations, and respect reduced-motion preferences for CSS UI effects.

## Documentation Standard

The README is a product surface, not only a developer note. It should show current gameplay screenshots, real feature scope, player-facing goals, controls, verification commands, and architecture map. Product screenshots must be regenerated from the live game when major UI, worldgen, or progression systems change.
